## The Historian`s Craft in Digital Humanities

In his famous book The Historian's Craft Marc Bloch (1992) says that historical documents “will speak only when they are properly questioned" (Bloch, 1992, p.53). As a historian in training, I see this observation as central to the readings on maps and countermapping. Katherine McDonough's “Maps as Data” and Crystal Nicole Eddins's “Fugitivity's and Future” examine how the questions researchers ask and the categories they use shape the knowledge digital methods produce. Making historical materials accessible to the public and to other disciplines is valuable, but researchers must also explain how they interpret those materials and what their evidence can support. The historian's responsibility to question sources continues through the construction of a database or a map. 

Digital methods also create opportunities to ask questions that emerge during research. By bringing large collections of documents into view together, they can reveal relationship that are difficult to notice when reading individual records. Yet a visible pattern still requires interpretation. Does a concentration on a map reflect a historical phenomenon, the uneven survival of documents, or the researcher's selection criteria? Bloch's insistence that inquiry remain flexible helps me understand this movement between questions and evidence (Bloch, 1992, p.54). A visualization can prompt us to return to our sources with new questions, but it cannot make those sources neutral. Selection, classification, and display all shape what we see. McDonough and Eddins show why we must examine these decisions as part of the research itself. 

McDonough (2024) gives this methodological responsibility a solid form by tracing three overlapping waves of digitations like scanning maps, manually creating data from their contents, and using machine learning to automate that work. Each opens different possibilities for historical inquiry. Automated methods allow researchers to investigate collections too large to examine map by map, potentially revealing patterns that prompt new questions. Yet McDonough asks, “can we trust data predicted by machines?” (para 10).  This question brings me back to the historian's craft. A pattern may reflect historical conditions, choices made by the original mapmakers, or errors introduced during automated classification. Making it visible does not establish what it means. Historians must examine how the data took shape and return to the sources to assess their interpretations. McDonough`s insistence that manual and automated methods serve different questions makes this an argument for choosing methods critically, with attention to what each can reveal and obscure. 

In “Fugitivity's and Futures": Black Studies in the Digital Era, “Eddins (2024) examines how digital scholarship can challenge the systems of surveillance that shaped slavery's archives. Her approach connects with Bloch's observation that historians can learn from sources beyond what their creators intended to communicate (Bloch, 1992, pp.52-53). An advertisement seeking the recapture of Julien, an enslaved in 1776, identifies his mother and sister, both free women, and their locations. The enslaver presented these relationships as clues for finding him. Eddings reads them as possible resources for securing refuge. This example makes Bloch's methodological point that the historian's questions can expose possibilities within a document that its author sought to suppress. Eddins adds a political purpose to that inquiry by asking how digital scholarship can foreground Black struggles for freedom. 

Her argument also builds on an established practice of reading slavery's archives for evidence of resistance. Enslavers published runaway advertisement to locate and recapture people who sought freedom. Historians can question these records to investigate how enslaved people resisted oppression, drew on their skills, and mobilized relationship to sustain their escape. Digital scholarship should carry this critical approach into the categories it creates and the narratives it presents. Eddins's analysis demonstrates why this matters information that enslavers recorded for surveillance can help researchers trace networks of solidarity and strategies of self-liberation. Yet evidence of escape does not necessarily establish lasting freedom. The challenge is to recognize resistance without claiming outcomes that the surviving records cannot confirm. For example, land and legacy similarly shows how researchers can use an institution`s own records to questions its public narrative. 

Such as interesting project created by UVA's 2019-2020 Praxis cohort, the project investigates university and UVA foundation expansion in Charlottesville and Albemarle County since the 1980s. It brings together property records, university meeting minutes, and newspaper coverage of community responses to examine the effects of that expansion. The project also acknowledges that its parcel datasets contain only the most recent transfers and sometimes lack transaction amounts (Dunkelberger et al.,2020). These limitations matter a map of ownership cannot, on its own, explain the full history of acquisition or how residents experienced these changes. Reading different sources together allows researchers to question institutional narratives while recognizing what the available evidence cannot establish. 

Alongside these questions about interpreting sources, generative AI creates a further problem image can appear to document events that never happened. False information has long circulated through the internet and social media, but AI makes convincing fabrications easier to produce. Jeremy Hsu's (2026) report on Google Earth illustrates this danger. Google briefly introduced a tool that allowed users to insert invented scenes into imaginary of real locations, then withdrew it following concerns about misinformation. A platform researchers use to verify information could therefore help produce misleading images. For historians and other social scientists, this makes it especially important to investigate how an image was made and whether independent evidence supports what it appears to show.
