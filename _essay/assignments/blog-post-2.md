## Machine Learning and Data Injustice

Can machine learning help us confront data injustice, or will it reproduce the inequalities that already shape access to knowledge? One of the promises that draws me to digital humanities is the possibility of making knowledge more accessible to wider publics. Project Gutenberg is one of the first examples that comes to mind: projects like it have opened new ways for people to encounter texts. Yet wider access still leaves questions about visibility. Whose works do these collections include, which languages do they prioritize, and whose knowledge remains difficult to find? Generative AI adds another dimension to these questions because it produces new text from patterns learned during training. The materials developers select and the decisions they make shape how a model represents the world. During Professor Patricia Murrieta-Flores’s lecture, I kept thinking about what happens when colonial assumptions inform those choices. An AI system may reproduce those assumptions in fluent, convincing language, making them difficult for readers to recognize and challenge.

Researchers can carry colonial assumptions into both digital and conventional methods. We choose questions, select sources, and decide how to classify what we find. A new tool will not change that approach on its own. Her proposed shift from inclusion to authority particularly interested me. Including a community’s knowledge in a collection does not necessarily give its members any control over how researchers interpret or use it. Confronting data injustice requires us to ask who makes those decisions and who has the power to challenge them.

During the workshop, my group examined Voyant Tools, and I tried it with my MA essay. Although I wrote the essay in English, I included many Turkish terms. Some results did not seem to reflect my text accurately, particularly where those terms appeared. I realized that I needed to examine the settings carefully and check the output against the essay itself. I could not determine from this exercise exactly what caused the problem, but it raised a useful question: text analysis for which texts, and in which languages? The Turkish expressions in my essay carry historical meanings central to my argument. How a tool handles them affects what I can learn from its results.

I remember raising a related question about Transkribus during one of our Tuesday discussions: how well do handwriting recognition models handle Arabic-script materials? As someone who works with Ottoman Turkish and hopes to study Arabic medical texts, I want these tools to accommodate the sources I need. Developing useful models requires expert transcriptions, suitable training data, funding, and computing infrastructure. Murrieta-Flores and Shanmugapriya T. (2026) explain how unequal resources and dominant technical standards shape whose knowledge computational systems can recognize. They also argue that building collections and preparing data already involve interpretation. This helped me see how decisions about investment and infrastructure influence historical research before anyone begins analyzing a corpus.

“Unlocking Colonial Records with Artificial Intelligence” offers a concrete example of how machine learning might address some of these barriers. Murrieta-Flores and her coauthors (2025) develop handwriting recognition models for sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Spanish colonial documents. Their approach could help users search document contents beyond brief catalogue descriptions and locate information about histories, lands, and languages. This makes me think about what similar investment could enable for Ottoman Turkish and Arabic sources. Making these materials searchable could help researchers discover evidence they might otherwise overlook, including researchers who would need translation or collaboration to interpret it. Broader access could also help challenge historical assumptions that draw on a narrow range of European sources. That change would still require researchers to engage critically with the newly accessible material. For me, this is an important possibility of machine learning: it can expand the range of evidence we encounter and help us ask questions that our existing source base has limited.

The lecture’s discussion of extraction also made me think about an NPR report on companies purchasing books for AI training and destroying the physical copies during scanning. Reporter Emanuel Maiberg traced a shipment to an Amazon facility and explained that “rare” books can include obscure publications with small print runs, such as specialized manuals (Ryan et al., 2026). As a history PhD student, I find this troubling. Materials that attract little attention today may help future researchers answer questions we have not yet imagined. Turning their contents into training data raises questions about preservation and control: who decides what matters, and who gains access to the knowledge that companies extract?

Together, the lecture, workshop, and readings pushed me to ask more of digital tools than whether they work quickly or produce useful results. Machine learning can help confront data injustice by making neglected collections searchable and supporting languages that receive less technical investment. Researchers and institutions also need to involve communities in defining projects, recognize the expertise behind training data, and sustain access to the resulting resources.
