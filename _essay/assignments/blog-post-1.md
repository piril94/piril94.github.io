## Mapping Evliya Çelebi’s Talismanic Istanbul

  Evliya Çelebi was a seventeenth-century Ottoman traveler and writer whose ten-volume Seyahatnâme (Book of Travels) records his journeys across the Ottoman world. Its first volume describes Istanbul, the city where he was born. In my MA essay, I examine his talisman narratives to understand how he connects natural dangers with supernatural forms of protection. In one story, damage to a column allows snakes to return; in another, the destruction of a monument leaves the city exposed to plague (Evliya Çelebi, 2006). These narratives also bring memories of Istanbul’s Roman and Byzantine past into Evliya Çelebi’s account of the Ottoman city. I am developing an ArcGIS StoryMap to explore where these stories take place and what their locations add to my reading.

  Two historical images have helped me think about how to present that history. Matrakçı Nasuh’s sixteenth-century depiction of Istanbul, from Beyân-ı Menâzil-i Sefer-i Irâkeyn (Description of the Stages of the Campaign in the Two Iraqs), shows Atmeydanı, the Ottoman name for the former Byzantine Hippodrome. Some monuments pictured there appear in Evliya Çelebi’s later talisman narratives. A view of Constantinople designed by Cristoforo Buondelmonti in the early fifteenth century offers an earlier image of the peninsula. Together, these images let me look at places and monuments across different periods. I would also need to move beyond them: Evliya Çelebi’s talisman narratives concern sites elsewhere in Istanbul, including along its shores (Matrakçı Nasuh, ca. 1537; Buondelmonti, ca. 1422).

  I plan to georeference suitable historical maps in ArcGIS Pro by matching identifiable places on a scanned map with locations on a referenced map. This could help me investigate changes in streets, shorelines, and place names. İrfan Dağdelen’s survey of Ottoman maps of Istanbul introduces maps that record urban features such as gardens, waterways, and streets (Dağdelen, 2015). I would use the images by Matrakçı Nasuh and Buondelmonti differently, as visual sources within the StoryMap. Their stylized views cannot be aligned point for point with a modern street grid.

  The StoryMap could contain a layer of sites identified in Evliya Çelebi’s text. For each site, I would provide his place name, the relevant passage, a proposed modern location, a citation, and a short explanation of how securely I can identify it. Readers could move between the mapped location, the text, and an image when one is available. A point on a modern map can look exact even when the historical evidence supports only an approximate location. Showing how I reached each identification matters as much as plotting the point itself.

  Can digital mapping open up new ways of asking historical questions? Mapping these sites could help me examine where Evliya Çelebi places different forms of danger and protection across Istanbul. It would also require me to explain the evidence behind each location. My MA essay will provide a foundation for my planned dissertation research on healthy gardens, where I hope to continue asking how people understood the relationship between their surroundings and health. I am also considering a separate 3D reconstruction of a garden associated with an Ottoman medical institution. SpatioScholar’s approach to linking 3D models with primary sources offers one possible model (SpatioScholar, n.d.). Historical maps might help estimate a garden’s location, while plans and written descriptions could inform its possible appearance. Any reconstruction would need to show which features come from evidence and which remain conjectural.
