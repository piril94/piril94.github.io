2.	The Open Islamicate Texts Initiative (Open ITI) develops digital text collections and improves optical character recognition (OCR) and handwritten text recognition (HTR) for Arabic- script sources. I chose this project because I work with Ottoman Turkish sources and hope to consult Arabic medical texts for my dissertation. Both accessing these materials and making them searchable would be valuable to my research. Since Arabic-script texts can be challenging for automated recognition systems, I find OpenITI`s investment in OCR and HTR particularly important. I am interested in how these tools could help me search for medical terms and compare passages across texts.  

https://openiti.org/about.html

   

3.	SpatioScholar is one of the projects I find most exciting and inspiring. Developed at the New Jersey Institute of Technology, it grew out of Dr. Burçak Özlüdil’s research on late Ottoman asylums in Istanbul. It uses Unity3D and MySQL to connect models with primary sources. According to the project’s aims, users can explore spaces, trace changes over time, and share annotations. I hope to study healthy gardens in my dissertation, and this project inspires me to combine historical maps and written descriptions to reconstruct their possible appearances and approximate locations, while showing the sources that inform my interpretation of these spaces.   


https://spatioscholar.njit.edu/index.html

4.	Ottoman History Podcast began in March 2011 and continues across multiple digital platforms. It features researchers discussing books and ongoing work across periods and themes in Ottoman studies. Its freely accessible Turkish and English episodes serve an important aim of digital humanities: bringing academic research to wider audiences. As a PhD student focusing on the history of science, I value discussions within my field alongside opportunities to discover other scholarship. Hearing researchers explain their sources and arguments broaden my perspective. The podcast also encourages me to consider how I could share my own research with people beyond an academic audience.

(https://www.ottomanhistorypodcast.com)

5.	Recogito is an open-source annotation tool first developed in 2014 as part of Pelagios. It allows users to annotate texts and images and link place names to geographical records. I chose it as a useful tool for organizing notes for the StoryMap I am developing. While reading Evliya Çelebi’s account of seventeenth-century Istanbul, I could record observations and connect passages to places. I could also use it when reading medical texts to annotate recipes, ingredients, and references to regions. Keeping these notes alongside their sources would help me return to relevant passages and bring them into my research and mapping.


(https://recogito.pelagios.org)   

6.	In “Humanities Approaches to Graphical Display,” Johanna Drucker examines how maps, charts, and other visualizations can make interpreted information appear objective and certain. She prefers “capta” to “data” because data suggests something already given, while capta emphasizes that researchers actively select, categorize, and interpret information. Her examples include John Snow’s cholera map. Although plotting deaths helped identify a source of the epidemic, each dot represented a person whose life and relationships could not be captured by that symbol alone. Drucker asks how the same landscape might look from the perspective of someone experiencing the epidemic. I chose this article because it connects with our class discussions about why data are never neutral. It also reminded me of our week on critical making: creating something involves decisions that we need to examine, and the process itself can help us think through a research question. For me, the historian’s craft remains central when using digital tools. We still need to question sources, evaluate evidence, and develop coherent historical arguments. Turning data into a map is not an end. As I develop my StoryMap, Drucker encourages me to reflect on what I select, how I represent it, and whether those choices help me answer my historical questions.

7.	In “Economic Geographies,” Antonis Hadjikyriacou uses GIS to map a 1572 Ottoman fiscal survey of Cyprus. He reminds us that just like written records, historical maps and digital visualizations are never neutral; they are loaded with biases, choices, and omissions. I chose this chapter because it reinforces that the historian's primary task source criticism must also apply to spatial history. This is crucial for my MA essay and StoryMap on Evliya Çelebi’s talismanic narratives in seventeenth-century Istanbul. As I examine the blurry line between the natural environment and the supernatural, Hadjikyriacou’s warning to "read between the pixels"  (p.261) guides me. It reminds me to constantly interrogate both my primary sources and the digital maps I create to represent them.

8.	In “Using Medieval and Early Modern Maps in Historical Research,” Leanna T. P. Brinkley demonstrates how historical maps can be analyzed as sources. Using a 1536 collection of portolan charts by Battista Agnese, she examines place names, navigational lines, language, materials, and differences in detail across regions to ask who made the maps, for whom, and for what purpose. This case study is relevant to my StoryMap because historical maps are essential sources for historians working with GIS. In the case of Istanbul, many maps were drawn by travelers, diplomats, and cartographers who came to the city for different reasons. Their maps can reveal how these visitors understood and represented Istanbul and the Ottoman Empire. Brinkley’s approach encourages me to examine the context and perspective behind the historical maps I use in my project.


9.	In “Reinterpreting Space: Mapping People and Relationships in Late Medieval and Early Modern English Cities Using GIS,” Justin Colson examines how historians can map social relationships from late medieval and early modern records. Using London deeds and wills, he shows how GIS can reveal patterns in relationships among witnesses, executors, and property owners. His discussion of the tension between the imprecision of historical sources and the apparent precision of GIS offers a useful methodological framework for my own work. It encourages me to consider how historical understandings of space can be translated into a modern mapping system without making the evidence seem more precise than it is. This is relevant to my StoryMap of Istanbul, where street names have changed since the early modern period and some locations are difficult to identify on a present-day map. Colson’s article helps me think through how to represent those locations and make the evidence behind my choices clear.

10.	In “Medieval to Modern: Using Spatial Data from the Digital Projects Icelandic Saga Map and Nafnið.is to Explore the Interaction between Narrative and Place in Iceland,” Emily Lethbridge considers how narratives and landscapes shape one another over time. This idea resonates with my research on Evliya Çelebi’s talisman stories about Istanbul. These stories preserve memories associated with the city’s ancient, Roman, and Byzantine past, which Evliya retells within an Ottoman framework. His accounts suggest that he knew Istanbul’s oral traditions well and adapted them to the world of his own time. I chose Lethbridge’s chapter because it helps me think about how stories carry different historical layers of meaning into the present. In my StoryMap, I hope to bring Evliya’s narratives into conversation with historical maps and the places they describe, making visible the city’s layered cultural memory.

  I developed this bibliography from questions connected to my research and my ArcGIS Story Maps project on Istanbul: how historians study place through digital methods, interpret historical maps, and connect texts to locations. To find articles and books, I searched the University of Virginia’s Virgo catalog using keywords such as “spatial history,” “spatial turn,” and “mapping.” I followed relevant references in the works I found, which led me to additional chapters and case studies on historical GIS, placenames, and narrative geography. I found digital projects through the Digital Ottoman Studies website and through general internet searches. After identifying each project, I consulted its website to learn about its aims, methods, and outputs. These projects include Seyyah (İSTKA), OpenITI, SpatioScholar, Recogito, and the Ottoman History Podcast. I chose them because they relate to my interests in Ottoman and Istanbul history, historical mapping, Arabic-script sources, and sharing scholarship with wider audiences. As I read across the articles, books, and project websites, I considered how each source could inform my work, from locating historical places to reading maps as evidence and exploring relationships between narrative and landscape. This process helped me balance practical project examples with methodological scholarship for my own ArcGIS Story Maps project.
