## Making with AI 

I have been a PhD student for about two and a half years, and a lot has changed in academia during this time. Zoom started as a temporary solution during the pandemic but became a permanent part of academic life. We began discussing a virtual academic experience, which neoliberal policies had already prepared the ground for as we already discussed . Now Artificial  Intelligence is also becoming part of our everyday lives. While we are still trying to understand these earlier changes, AI brings new concerns about the future of academia. Some academics want to ban AI altogether, while others think there is no going back and argue that we should focus on using it ethically. While I understand the educators who want to ban it, I think AI can offer us new ideas and solutions if we use it carefully and avoid presenting its work as our own.

I think this is where Prof. Emily K. Johnson and Prof. Anastasia Salter’s visit to UVA last week comes in. Through different examples, they showed us how we can think about the connection between AI and critical making. Over lunch on Thursday, we discussed AI and visited makerspaces together. Friday was particularly interesting because we tried some of the applications they had mentioned in their latest books. The highlight was the first “hypertext” activity, where we used a piece of string to connect words. This helped us understand Twine, which they introduced later. I also tried writing a short piece about the historical gardens of Istanbul, the subject of my PhD research, and linking specific words together.

Twine and the other applications helped us when we put in the work ourselves. They did not do everything for us from scratch. For me, this is also central to the AI discussion. We can discuss ideas with AI and let it offer suggestions, but we should make the final decisions and take responsibility for what we create. I do not want it to generate the whole piece for me. Critical making connects to these choices at every stage of our work. In the hypertext activity with string, I decided which words and sentences to connect. When we moved to Twine, the program helped me put those connections into practice.

As I remember it, someone in the audience suggested sea monsters, and Prof. Salter used that idea in a prompt for Codex. Within about twenty to twenty-five minutes, we saw a simple game take shape. This was one of the most interesting activities. I wish we had focused on just two or three activities so we could spend more time exploring each one.

A few weeks before the critical making workshop, we had an assignment to try 3D printing. I wanted to print the monuments of Istanbul’s Hippodrome, which I discuss in my MA essay. I could only get the base from TerraPrinter, so I searched for models elsewhere. Some were expensive or inaccurate, and I did not feel comfortable downloading files from unfamiliar sites. I then tried Gemini, which produced three STL files. After asking ChatGPT for a second opinion and discussing the problems with Gemini, I decided to continue with ChatGPT.

The models were not entirely accurate, but I thought that I could use them for the printing exercise without adjusting their size before printing. The Serpent Column model, for example, did not show the intertwined snakes clearly. So I had only written “Serpent Column” in my prompt. Describing the snake forms more carefully might have produced a different result, though I would still need to compare it with photographs and measurements. Looking back after the workshop, I can see how my choices shaped the process, from choosing the monuments to deciding which inaccuracies I could accept for this exercise.
